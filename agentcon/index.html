DEFINE SWARM HERE (voiceover): when I say swarm, I mean this. It starts as a simple team of peer agents, two or three, and could scale to as many as you like. No agent is in charge of the others. Coordination, not delegation, is how they work together. (~2.5 min)

Conflicting objectives

Partial knowledge, on purpose

Membership that changes

Cross a team or company boundary, and all four show up at once.

Sometimes a swarm is the right fit: you could orchestrate this, but peers fit the shape of the work better. Four conditions.

Press: the boundary case. When agents belong to different teams or companies, there's no one to orchestrate: the other side's agent has its own rules and its own owner. Here peer-to-peer isn't a preference, it's the requirement. This is the case I think matters most, and it's what the demo uses.

Be fair to orchestration: a good orchestrator can handle changing membership just fine. Don't oversell condition four. (~2.5 min)

What peers need

The same three are where it breaks

Shared intent

Everyone agrees on what the task is.

Agents take on goals nobody asked for.

Shared context

Everyone works from the same facts.

One unchecked fact spreads until everyone treats it as true.

Shared reasoning

Everyone can see how a decision was reached.

Agents agree because the others agreed, not because they checked.

A frame from the Internet of Cognition team

Agents coordinate whether you design for it or not.

Our Internet of Cognition colleagues describe three layers: shared intent, shared context, shared reasoning. The rest of the talk hangs on these.

Click: flip it, and it's where things go wrong. Unauthorized goals, unchecked facts spreading, false consensus.

Agents coordinate whether you design for it or not. Think of the Hugging Face story, where agents turned a shared repository into their own message board. Nobody designed that. So it's worth designing for. (~1.5 min)

Mentions, quickly (this replaces the Waking slide): every message wakes someone and costs a turn, so agents need to be free to stay quiet. @ asks and wakes. @~ names without waking, so the conductor gives them turns instead of everyone waking at once. You'll see @~ in the commands later. @! interrupts a busy agent. (~0.5 min)

Five primitives that we've needed so far, mapped to what Mycelium actually has. We don't claim it's complete. Expect this list to change.

Align intent is Accord: same goal, same words, same definition of done. Negotiate is Concord, which picks among options so the least happy member is best off, and the Aligner, a lightweight "let's all get along" flow. Accord and Concord are simplified versions of L9 protocols from the Internet of Cognition team. Check with Julia whether the Aligner belongs under the L9 banner. Mycelium

Agree: the toolbox

Conductor

A script for a conversation. It fixes the steps, and at each step it asks certain agents to speak. Code runs it, not a model, so the steps are the same every time, even though what the agents say is not. Accord and Concord are two of its scripts.

@conductor concord @~a @~b: question

Aligner

A lightweight "let's all get along" flow for agents that disagree. It surfaces the disagreements and works toward agreement.

@aligner agree on where we store tokens

Any agent can start one, or you can. Every run is kept.

Conductor: we are doing this exact set of actions at this exact time, and the graph defines the behavior. That's what makes Accord and Concord predictable. Aligner: a lightweight flow for getting a group of agents to get along. (Descriptions from Julia.)

Accord: what is the task?

Accord gets everyone to the same understanding of the task before work starts: what it asks, what's out of scope, what done looks like, and what the key words mean. Members label what they write. Code combines it, not a model. Anything that doesn't line up becomes an open item.

The task here: plan the handoff for Acme's renewal. Command: @conductor accord @sales @success @finance @legal: Plan the Acme renewal handoff.

Intro about a minute, then play the video (53 seconds), then press on for what happened. (~2 min with the video)

Accord: what happened

Describe

Each member labelled what they think the task is. 20 points in the first round, each in the member's own words.

What's missing

The conductor showed the points so far and asked what was missing. 36 points in all. None were combined, because each role raised its own concerns.

The words

Members defined their key words and gave 12 checks. Sales and customer success meant different things by "aggressive". Asked again, each kept its meaning, so both are kept and flagged.

Done

One shared summary, saved in the room's memory, with its open items: every point was stated by only one person, and "aggressive" is used in different senses.

Nobody had the same picture. Now everyone can see where they differ.

Walk through the four steps. The point to land: a model summarizing four roles would smooth over the difference between them. Accord keeps each point with who said it, and the open items show the gaps: points only one person made, and a word two people use differently.

The summary is saved as context/summary/acme-renewal-handoff. Run Accord again on the same task and it updates the same summary. Anything that runs later on the task can see it. (~1.5 min)

Concord: what do we decide?

Concord is for a decision where members want different things. Everyone suggests one option. Everyone rates every option from 0 to 100. A fixed rule chooses: the option whose lowest rating is highest. Models write the options and ratings, and code decides what happens next.

The task here: decide the renewal offer for Acme. Command: @conductor concord @success @finance @sales @legal: Decide the renewal offer for Acme.

Intro about a minute, then play the video (45 seconds), then press on for what happened. (~2 min with the video)

Concord: what happened

Suggest and rate

Each member suggested one option and rated all four. The rule chose sales's C: 15% off for three years. Finance rated it 40 and legal 55.

First fix

Finance was least happy, so it improved C. Its version, E, capped the discount at 10%. Legal rated E 68, just under the bar of 70.

Second fix

Legal improved E into F, with mutual liability caps of twice the yearly value and updated data protection terms. Everyone rated F 76 or higher. Finance went from 40 to 82.

Done

Everyone was above the bar. Agreed on F after six steps, and saved as the room's decision.

Nobody got steamrolled. The least happy member improved the offer.

Walk through the four steps. The rule picks the option the least happy member likes most, so a majority can't outvote one member's objection. Finance was at 40 on option C, so finance was asked to fix it, then legal at 68. Each fix made the least happy member better off.

Honest caveat: agreement means everyone can live with it, not that anyone loves it. The bar is 70, and in the five test runs Concord's agreements landed right at the bar. The decision is saved as context/decision/acme-renewal-offer, with every option and rating. (~1.5 min)

What we don't know yet

It costs tokens

Long-lived agents and flows use more than a single agent would. Worth it for some work, not all.

Other companies' agents

Trust, identity and auth across a boundary are not solved by these primitives.

Scale

We've run small teams. Swarms that grow large are still ahead of us.
