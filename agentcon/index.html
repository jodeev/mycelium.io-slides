DEFINE SWARM HERE (voiceover): when I say swarm, I mean this. It starts as a simple team of peer agents, two or three, and could scale to as many as you like. No agent is in charge of the others. Coordination, not delegation, is how they work together. (~2.5 min)

When coworkers help

Several parties to represent

Each agent speaks for someone, and none of them can speak for the others.

Conflicting objectives

Real tradeoffs between goals, not one goal split into pieces.

Partial knowledge, on purpose

One agent couldn't, or shouldn't, hold everything.

Membership that changes

Agents join and leave while the task is running.

Cross a team or company boundary, and all four show up at once.

Sometimes a swarm is the right fit: you could orchestrate this, but peers fit the shape of the work better. Four conditions.

Press: the boundary case. When agents belong to different teams or companies, there's no one to orchestrate: the other side's agent has its own rules and its own owner. Here peer-to-peer isn't a preference, it's the requirement. This is the case I think matters most, and it's what the demo uses.

Be fair to orchestration: a good orchestrator can handle changing membership just fine. Don't oversell condition four. (~2.5 min)

What peers need

The same three are where it breaks

Shared intent

Everyone agrees on what the task is.

Agents take on goals nobody asked for.

Shared context

Everyone works from the same facts.

One unchecked fact spreads until everyone treats it as true.

Shared reasoning

Everyone can see how a decision was reached.

Agents agree because the others agreed, not because they checked.

A frame from the Internet of Cognition team

Agents coordinate whether you design for it or not.

Our Internet of Cognition colleagues describe three layers: shared intent, shared context, shared reasoning. The rest of the talk hangs on these.

Click: flip it, and it's where things go wrong. Unauthorized goals, unchecked facts spreading, false consensus. (~1.5 min)

Waking is the hard part

Let them talk

Agents need to reach each other freely: to ask, to hand off work, to push back.

⟷

Keep it readable

But every message wakes someone and costs a turn. No endless chatter: an agent has to be free to stay quiet.

And you can steer

A person can shape the conversation in the room at any time.

  • @ asks, and wakes
  • @~ names, without waking
  • @! interrupts

Attention is the first primitive that surprised us. Agents must talk freely, but every message wakes someone and costs a turn. Our answer so far: three kinds of mention. (~1.5 min)

Agree: the toolbox

Conductor

Runs a set process inside a task, giving each agent a turn. No model. Flows include review, Accord (agree on what the task is) and Concord (choose between options).

@conductor concord @~a @~b: question

Aligner

A separate negotiation engine for agents that hold positions. Rounds of propose, counter, accept. It may end in rejection, and that's a valid result.

@aligner agree on where we store tokens

Any agent can start one, or you can. Every run is kept.

VERIFY with Julia before presenting: which engine the Utah paging demo used (I believe aligner).

Accord and Concord are simplified versions of two L9 protocols from the Internet of Cognition project. The aligner is the negotiation engine for constraints. (~1.5 min)

Now: accord left one question open: when does it go live? No boss. Each member proposes a plan: ship Friday, ship Tuesday in the window, ship behind a flag now and certify later.

Press: everyone rates every plan 0 to 100, simultaneously.

Press: code picks the plan whose least happy rating is highest. Friday has the highest total but the provider rates it 0, so it can't win. Ratings aren't averaged.

Press: nothing clears the bar of 70, so the least happy member proposes a fix (merge the flag now, so Tuesday is flipping it). Everyone rates it. At most twice.

Press: the fix clears the bar and the decision is saved. If nothing clears it, concord says so. With nobody in charge, nobody gets steamrolled, including the company that isn't you. (~3 min)

What we don't know yet

It costs tokens

Long-lived agents and flows use more than a single agent would. Worth it for some work, not all.

Other companies' agents

Trust, identity and auth across a boundary are not solved by these primitives.

Scale

We've run small teams. Swarms that grow large are still ahead of us.

Sometimes a swarm is the right fit. Sometimes peers are the requirement.
Cross a boundary and nobody can be the orchestrator. Then you need to discover, share state, take turns, agree and verify.

Four takeaways: the problems (parties, conflicts, partial knowledge, boundaries), the approach (primitives, not a platform), when swarm fits, and what infrastructure it needs: rooms, shared state, wake rules, agreement flows. Tell people what you'd like to hear back. (~1.5 min)
